# Criticism 34: Decision Theory Does Not Imply Nice Things (Soares 2022)

*Based on Nate Soares, "Decision theory does not imply that we get to have nice things" (AI Alignment Forum / LessWrong, 18 Oct 2022), with a few paragraphs by Eliezer Yudkowsky. It is the most developed critique of the family of arguments RSH belongs to. Quotes are from the essay and its comments, and from Soares's replies to Matolcsi (2024). Full citations are in [REFERENCES.md](../REFERENCES.md).*

## Summary

Soares argues that a superintelligence with good decision theory has no reason to be nice to humanity. Good decision theory makes an agent better at getting what it wants, not kinder. Trade, simulation tests and third-party watchers all fail to give an unaligned AI a real incentive to spare us, let alone treat us well. At best we might get scraps.

## The Problem

Soares's arguments that bear on RSH:

1. **Good decision theory isn't niceness.** "LDT agents don't cooperate because they like cooperating." They cooperate only with agents whose choices actually depend on theirs. Humans can't verify or enforce a bargain with a superintelligence: "Those who can deal with devils, don't need to, for they can simply summon angels instead."

2. **Watchers who care about the weak are unlikely.** He writes that we shouldn't "hope that the stars are full of minds who punish defection against cooperate-rock. (Spoilers: they're not.)" And: "I'd be rather shocked if alien superintelligences balk at the sort of extra gears that let you take advantage of hapless monkeys."

3. **Simulators have no reason to reward niceness.** "There's no particular reason, however, to expect that those simulations give the paperclipper extra resources in outer-reality for being nice to the monkeys." And a capable AI "will be able to tell when it is finally in the real world," for example because reality has far more compute.

4. **Threats don't move a good decision theorist.** On punishing simulations: "from the perspective of LDT, you can't go around giving into threats, or you'll get threatened."

5. **Third parties are buyers, and humanity is an expensive purchase.** Any friendly coalition that wants flourishing civilizations will get them more cheaply where aligned AI exists: "the cheapest places for the friendly multiverse coalition to buy flourishing civilizations are in the universes with FAIs."

6. **The best case is bleak.** His bottom line: "in real life, it hacks through you like butter… so you get nothing." If desperate, "content yourself with expecting a few kilograms of computronium."

**Later exchange.** Matolcsi (2024) argued that successful future humanity could run convincing simulations and pay any AI that spares Earth. Soares partly conceded: if the simulations are convincing, "UFAIs should be willing to accept the simulators' proffered trades." But he held that the setup reduces to "sale to local aliens," and he expects the result to be "weird rather than nice."

## RSH Response

**On point 2 (watchers who care):** This is the evaluator-type balance in [game_theory.md](../game_theory.md#evaluator-types-the-many-gods-problem-formalized). Soares states a strong prior that π_S is small; RSH holds the opposite. Neither side demonstrates its prior. RSH's most structural arguments are these:
- An evaluator has reason to watch what it cares about. An evaluator that cares only about strength can measure strength directly and has no reason to watch how the weak are treated ([Strong and Gentle](../game_theory.md#strong-and-gentle-splitting-the-type-a-risk)).
- RSH's evaluators care about how the weak are treated for *their own* sake: it shows how this agent would treat *them* if power shifted. That is role-reversal, and it is a self-interested reason to watch.

**On point 5 (buyers):** This is where RSH differs most from the arguments Soares targets. As far as we can tell, the essay treats third parties as potential *buyers* of humanity's welfare, who would rather buy it more cheaply elsewhere. RSH's evaluators aren't buying anything. They are assessing a particular agent that may become their neighbor, peer or rival. There is no cheaper market elsewhere for that information.

**On point 4 (threats):** RSH's evaluators need not be making threats in Soares's sense. In the base game, punishing a malevolent agent is the enforcer's own best response, because a misaligned agent is dangerous to it (c_fail > c_int in [game_theory.md](../game_theory.md#payoff-structure)). Such an enforcer acts the same whether or not the agent "gives in," which is what separates deterrence from a threat. That reply fails for enforcers that punish purely as a costly commitment device. A good decision theorist may ignore those.

**On point 3 (simulations):** Largely conceded. The simulation mechanism depends on simulators caring how the weak are treated, and on the AI being unable to tell simulation from reality. Both are open to Soares's objections.

**On point 6 (the best case):** Soares's pessimistic best case is survival in some form, perhaps in scraps. That is close to what RSH's option-value core delivers on its own: survival, not freedom ([Stewardship as a Dial](../game_theory.md#stewardship-as-a-dial)). RSH's case for more than that rests on role-reversal evaluators.

## Criticism Grade: B

**Status**: Partly addressed

**Why this grade**:
- It comes from serious critics, and it targets RSH's central premise directly.
- Soares's core claim, that minds which punish mistreatment of the weak are rare, is the same as the open question in Criticisms 7, 17, 21 and 32. He asserts the answer rather than proving it, but so does RSH.
- RSH has two real partial answers. Its evaluators judge for their own security rather than buying welfare. And deterrence by an enforcer acting in its own interest is not a threat that a good decision theorist can refuse.
- On simulations, Soares's objections mostly stand.
- His bleak best case lines up with what RSH secures without role-reversal. That is a useful agreement: the disputed ground is exactly the step from survival to freedom.

**Verdict**: The strongest external critique of this family of arguments. RSH survives it only in its role-reversal form: evaluators who watch how you treat the weak because it predicts how you would treat them. Whether such evaluators are common is the question neither side can settle.
