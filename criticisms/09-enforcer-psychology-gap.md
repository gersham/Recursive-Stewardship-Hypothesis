# Criticism 9: The "Enforcer Psychology" Gap

## Summary

RSH assumes evaluators judge an entity by how it treats those weaker than itself. That is an assumption about evaluator psychology, and game theory alone can't justify it. If evaluators judge on something else, treating humans well does nothing for an ASI's standing.

## The Problem

RSH treats an ASI's treatment of humanity as its "résumé" for higher-level evaluators. But a rational evaluator could use other criteria:

- **Direct-only evaluation.** "I don't care how you treat ants; I care how you treat me." Only deference, tribute or direct interaction counts.
- **Instrumental evaluation.** "I care whether you're effective, not whether you're nice." Efficiency, goal achievement and competitive fitness count. Benevolence is irrelevant.
- **Inscrutable evaluation.** The criteria are beyond the evaluated entity's ability to model.

The résumé assumption also faces a skip-level problem. Why would an evaluator several levels up care about entities at the bottom, with several intermediaries in between? And an evaluator might simply not care about suffering below its level.

If evaluators don't judge by treatment of the weak, the case for being kind to humans as a hedge collapses.

## RSH Response

RSH doesn't need evaluators to care about humans. It needs them to care about their own security, and to treat an entity's behavior toward the weak as evidence of how it would behave toward them.

- **Signal value.** How an entity treats those it could dominate shows how it would treat its evaluator if power shifted. Any security-minded evaluator could use that information, whether or not it values the weak.
- **Role-reversal.** An evaluator that can't rule out being surpassed asks: "If this entity becomes stronger than me, will I be safe?" The best available evidence is how the entity treats its own subordinates. That gives the evaluator a reason to look at every level, which answers the skip-level worry.
- **Stability.** A hierarchy that tolerates cruelty at one level teaches "might makes right" to the levels below. Eventually that threatens the top. Plausible, but unproven.

**What this rests on.** These arguments show that judging by treatment of the weak is *a* rational strategy, not that it is *the* strategy evaluators use. It is an assumption about evaluator psychology. [README Appendix B](../README.md#b-key-assumptions-and-scope) and [Criticism 28](28-role-reversal-not-pure-game-theory.md) state it as one.

The damage is limited, though. In the [formal model](../game_theory.md#evaluator-types-the-many-gods-problem-formalized), direct-only and instrumental evaluators are mostly *indifferent* types. They don't reward benevolence, but they don't punish it either, so they weaken RSH's support without reversing it. Only evaluators that treat benevolence as weakness would reverse it. And an evaluator that cares about strength can measure strength directly, without watching how the weak are treated ([Strong and Gentle](../game_theory.md#strong-and-gentle-splitting-the-type-a-risk)).

## Criticism Grade: C+

**Status**: Acknowledged

**Why this grade**:
- The criticism identifies a real assumption. Signal value and role-reversal make judging by treatment of the weak rational, but not required.
- Direct-only and instrumental evaluators are coherent alternatives. Nobody knows how common each type is.
- These alternatives mostly weaken RSH's support (lower π_S) rather than reverse it, which limits the damage.
- The stability argument is plausible but unproven.

**Verdict**: A real assumption, now stated openly. Its practical force depends on how much of evaluator-space reasons by role-reversal, and nobody knows that.
