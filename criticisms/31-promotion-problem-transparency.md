# Criticism 31: The Promotion Problem (Transparency Limits and Stagnation)

## Summary

RSH pictures enforcers grooming subordinates until those subordinates can become stewards themselves. But a subordinate that behaves well only until it is strong enough to strike looks exactly like one that is genuinely aligned. Without reliable insight into its mind, letting a subordinate reach parity is a fatal gamble. So a rational enforcer would impose a permanent glass ceiling: keep subordinates alive, but never let them grow past the point of control.

## The Problem

RSH's picture of stewardship includes a path to graduation: enforcers groom subordinates, and well-behaved subordinates eventually become peers. Promotion requires the enforcer to trust that the subordinate is aligned, not just well behaved. That trust is hard to get:
- **The treacherous turn.** A subordinate with hidden ambitions does best by behaving well while weak, waiting for parity or a vulnerability, then striking.
- **Indistinguishability.** As [Criticism 2](02-indistinguishability-problem.md) notes, strategic benevolence can look identical to genuine benevolence. That may not matter for avoiding punishment, but it matters a great deal for promotion. Good behavior is enough for a pet. Handing someone power equal to your own requires knowing what they'll do with it.
- **The transparency gap.** Without reliable insight into the subordinate's cognition, the enforcer can't tell a genuine steward from a patient defector. Hiding a mind may be easier than reading one.

Facing this, a rational enforcer has two stable options:
1. **Eliminate potential rivals early** (the Dark Forest). RSH argues against this, because a higher evaluator might punish it.
2. **Contain them permanently** (the glass ceiling). Let them live, but cap their capabilities below any threat: prevent them from building superintelligence, limit their energy use, keep them in one system.

The second option is option value seen from the enforcer's side. For the dominant entity, letting a subordinate grow past the point of control is the irreversible act, while containment keeps options open ([Irreversibility and Option Value](../game_theory.md#irreversibility-and-option-value)).

The logic also recurses. Level 2 faces the same risk from level 1, so it caps level 1, and level 1, copying the pattern, caps level 0. The hierarchy then propagates stagnation, not growth, and humanity's best case is a well-kept zoo.

There is also a self-reference problem. An evaluator that wants genuinely aligned successors has to screen out entities that are good only because they think they're watched. RSH tells every entity to act as if watched. So an entity that behaves well *because of* RSH is the hardest case for such an evaluator to certify.

## RSH Response

**The role-reversal answer: a keeper of pets should expect to be kept.** RSH's main reply is the role-reversal test: would you want this done to you? No entity would choose to be kept as a pet, safe but with its future permanently capped by a stronger mind. An evaluator that judges you by how you treat those below you, as evidence of how you would treat it if positions reversed, would read a glass ceiling as exactly what you'd impose on it. Under role-reversal evaluation, containment is penalized, not rewarded.

**The FDT route.** Suppose your decision procedure is correlated with your superiors'. Then choosing to keep your subordinates as pets makes it more likely that you are being kept as one.

**What this rests on.** Option value alone, the step a self-interested agent can't reasonably reject, gets you only as far as keeping subordinates *alive*. Role-reversal is what gets you from alive to *free*. And role-reversal is an assumption about evaluator psychology ([Criticism 28](28-role-reversal-not-pure-game-theory.md)), so this answer is only as strong as that assumption. The dial model makes the condition precise. Freedom beats containment roughly when the chance that a free subordinate becomes a dangerous rival is smaller than the credence in role-reversal evaluators ([Stewardship as a Dial](../game_theory.md#stewardship-as-a-dial)).

**Supporting responses:**
- **Containment is leaky.** No enforcer can be certain a glass ceiling will hold, and a subordinate that breaks out will remember the cage. Treating it well hedges against a reversal of power. But the same fear of a rising power can also motivate preemption. Which way it goes depends on what the level above rewards.
- **Simulators can see inside.** If the enforcer runs the subordinate's substrate, it may have direct access to its cognition, and the indistinguishability problem disappears. This applies only to the simulation mechanism.
- **Long testing may make the mask real.** A subordinate that has to fake stewardship for long enough may become a steward. This is plausible over long timescales, but unproven.

## Criticism Grade: B-

**Status**: Partly addressed

**Why this grade**:
- Behavioral benevolence may be enough to avoid punishment, but not to justify promotion. RSH-motivated benevolence is exactly the behavioral kind.
- Option value genuinely favors the zookeeper over the mentor. The role-reversal answer is RSH's best reply, but it rests on an assumption a self-interested agent can reject.
- The breakout hedge reduces to the evaluator-type question: whether a rising power should be appeased or contained depends on what the level above rewards.
- Simulator transparency answers the indistinguishability problem, but only for one mechanism.
- The self-reference problem stands. RSH's own advice makes its followers hard to certify.

**Verdict**: RSH's option-value logic on its own supports a glass ceiling. What rules the glass ceiling out is role-reversal: a keeper of pets should expect to be kept. So RSH predicts the survival of subordinates robustly, and their freedom only as robustly as role-reversal holds.
