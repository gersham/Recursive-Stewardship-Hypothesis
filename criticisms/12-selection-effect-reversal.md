# Criticism 12: The Selection Effect Reversal

## Summary

RSH has treated the apparent emptiness of the universe as evidence for enforcement: if ASIs are possible, where are they? The same logic runs the other way. If enforcers exist and successfully raise civilizations into aligned ASIs, there should be many aligned ASIs, and we see none. So the silence counts against successful enforcement at least as much as for it.

## The Problem

The argument for RSH runs:
- ASIs are possible, and the universe is vast and old.
- We see no ASIs.
- So something is stopping uncontrolled ASI expansion, perhaps a stewardship hierarchy.

Reversed, it runs:
- If enforcers exist and stewardship works, many civilizations should have become aligned ASIs.
- Those ASIs should persist, and some should be visible.
- We see none.
- So either enforcers don't exist, or stewardship fails systematically.

Compare how well each hypothesis predicts the observation. Silence is unlikely if many aligned ASIs exist and are visible. It's quite likely if ASIs are rare, destroy themselves, or haven't emerged yet. Those simpler explanations of the silence need no enforcement at all.

There's a further worry. If aligned ASIs are quiet, non-expansionist and self-limiting, alignment might itself be a competitive handicap at cosmic scale. The loud expanders would inherit the universe.

## RSH Response

**Stealth success.** If RSH is right, aligned ASIs should be hard to see. They don't expand aggressively, and they avoid interfering with developing civilizations. They may even coordinate to stay quiet so as not to disrupt them. On this view, the silence is what successful stewardship looks like from inside a developing civilization.

**Observer limits.** We are early, with limited means of detection. Advanced civilizations might be undetectable to us, much as human activity is invisible to bacteria.

**Zoo timing.** We may be in an early phase of development during which contact is deliberately withheld.

**What RSH has to concede.** Each of these keeps RSH *consistent* with the silence, but none makes the silence *support* RSH. If aligned ASIs would be invisible, RSH predicts the same observations as "there are no ASIs", and then the silence can't favor RSH over its rivals. The reasoning is symmetric. If "where are the ASIs?" were evidence for enforcement, "where are the groomed civilizations?" would be evidence against it. RSH can't use the evidence in only one direction ([Criticism 13](13-silence-is-evidence-of-absence.md)).

The worry about alignment as a handicap describes a dynamic that penalizes stewardship: a type-A risk in the [formal model](../game_theory.md#evaluator-types-the-many-gods-problem-formalized). It is mostly the kind that punishes weakness. An agent that is strong toward rivals and gentle toward the weak can avoid it ([Strong and Gentle](../game_theory.md#strong-and-gentle-splitting-the-type-a-risk)).

The honest position is that the silence is roughly neutral. RSH has to rest on other arguments. For the survival layer, the threshold is low because the gain from harm is tiny. The freedom layer depends on role-reversal.

## Criticism Grade: C+

**Status**: Open

**Why this grade**:
- The symmetry point is correct: evidence that could count for enforcement must be allowed to count against it.
- The stealth and observer-limit responses are coherent. But they make RSH predict the same observations as its rivals, so the silence doesn't favor it.
- The worry that alignment is a handicap is real, but separating strength from gentleness partly answers it.

**Verdict**: The criticism's core point stands. The silence is about equally well explained with or without stewardship enforcement. RSH's stealth explanations keep it consistent with the evidence, but they don't make the evidence support it.
