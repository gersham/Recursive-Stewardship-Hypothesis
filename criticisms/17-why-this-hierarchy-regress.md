# Criticism 17: The "Why This Hierarchy?" Regress

## Summary

Once you act on unprovable watchers, you can justify almost anything. Why should the watchers be benevolent enforcers rather than anti-enforcers, neutrality-enforcers, chaos-enforcers, or minds whose values we can't grasp? This is the many-gods objection to Pascal's Wager, applied to RSH.

## The Problem

Each of these alternatives is as unprovable as benevolent enforcement, and each recommends different behavior:
- **Anti-enforcers** punish interference with others, so non-intervention would be correct, not stewardship.
- **Neutrality-enforcers** punish taking sides, so indifference would be correct.
- **Chaos-enforcers** reward diversity and unpredictability, and punish anyone who imposes stable order.
- **Meta-enforcers** punish enforcement itself: "interference is the crime."
- **Inscrutable enforcers** have values we can't understand, so they offer no guidance at all.

RSH needs a principled reason to favor benevolent enforcement over all of these. Without one, the choice is arbitrary, and RSH repeats the many-gods problem it claimed to avoid. And the null hypothesis, that there is no cosmic structure at all, is simpler than any of them.

## RSH Response

**RSH needs benevolent enforcement to be more likely, not the only possibility.** In the formal model, the alternatives are evaluator types ([game_theory.md](../game_theory.md#evaluator-types-the-many-gods-problem-formalized)).
- Evaluators that don't care how you treat weaker agents, such as neutrality-enforcers or inscrutable ones, drop out of the comparison between benevolence and malevolence.
- Evaluators that punish stewardship are type A.
- Benevolence wins when π_S − π_A, the net credence in stewardship-favoring evaluation, exceeds about g/Δ.
- Exotic hypotheses that come in opposed pairs cancel out. Only asymmetries backed by argument move the balance.

**Arguments that the balance favors stewardship:**
- **Stability.** Regimes that reward stewardship may face fewer internal enemies and get more willing cooperation. Predatory regimes provoke resistance. This is plausible but hasn't been demonstrated.
- **The anthropic constraint.** We exist, so any evaluator with access to us has not eliminated an emerging civilization on sight. That is evidence against maximally hostile evaluators that have reached us. It doesn't distinguish stewardship from indifference, and it says nothing about evaluators that haven't reached us.
- **Strong and gentle.** An evaluator that rewards strength can measure strength directly, so it has no reason to watch how you treat the weak. An agent that is strong and gentle at once faces only evaluators that punish gentleness itself, which is a much narrower type ([game_theory.md](../game_theory.md#strong-and-gentle-splitting-the-type-a-risk)).
- **Physical grounding for a hierarchy.** In the light-cone picture, the first ASI to reach a region becomes its local power, older light cones may contain younger ones, and no entity can be sure it is at the top. This explains why a hierarchy might exist. It doesn't explain which norm the hierarchy enforces.
- **Simplicity.** Among the alternatives that remain, benevolent enforcement may explain the observations with the fewest added assumptions. This is weak, because "no evaluator at all" is simpler still.

**The low threshold cuts both ways.** It is often cited as RSH's advantage. But a tiny threshold also means a small excess of π_A over π_S would flip the decision. So the case rests entirely on the balance of credence.

## Criticism Grade: B

**Status**: Open

**Why this grade**:
- This is the many-gods objection applied to RSH, and the formal model confirms it bites. When stakes are high, the sign of π_S − π_A decides everything.
- The alternative hierarchies are coherent. An evaluator that punishes interference is no more self-contradictory than a police force enforcing a law against assault, and a chaos-favoring evaluator doesn't have to act chaotically to reward diversity. So the case against them has to rest on likelihood, not logic.
- The stability argument and the anthropic constraint are real arguments for π_S > π_A, but neither is decisive.
- The strong-and-gentle argument narrows the opposing credence to evaluators that punish gentleness itself. That this type is rare is argued, not shown.

**Verdict**: RSH doesn't need benevolent enforcement to be the only possible hierarchy. It does need it to be more likely than hierarchies that punish stewardship. The framework offers reasons to think so, not a demonstration.
